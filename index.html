About Diwali · दीपावली

Diwali, or Deepavali ("a row of lamps"), is the festival of lights, celebrated by Hindus, Jains, Sikhs and Buddhists in India and around the world. It celebrates the victory of light over darkness, good over evil and knowledge over ignorance.

Why is it celebrated?
  • Lord Rama comes home. In the Ramayana, Rama returns to Ayodhya with Sita and Lakshmana after 14 years of exile and the defeat of Ravana. The people light rows of diyas to welcome them.
  • Goddess Lakshmi. Families pray to Lakshmi, goddess of wealth and well-being, and Lord Ganesha, and keep their homes clean and bright to welcome her.
  • Lord Krishna and Narakasura. In many parts of India, especially the south, the festival marks Krishna's victory over the demon Narakasura.
  • Kali Puja. In West Bengal, Odisha and Assam, the same night is devoted to Goddess Kali.
  • Jains mark the nirvana of Lord Mahavira, and Sikhs celebrate Bandi Chhor Divas, when Guru Hargobind was freed from prison.
  • For many traders it is also the start of a new financial year, with new account books (bahi-khata).
When is it, and which calendar?

Diwali follows the Hindu lunisolar calendar. Lakshmi Puja falls on Amavasya, the new-moon night, of the month of Kartik. (In the amanta calendar used in the south and west, the same night ends the month of Ashvin.) That is why it moves around in the Gregorian calendar, landing between mid-October and mid-November, and why the night sky in this app has no moon.

It is a five-day festival. In 2026:

Fri 6 NovDhanteras: buying gold, silver or new utensils for good fortune
Sat 7 NovNaraka Chaturdashi (Choti Diwali): early bath, first lamps
Sun 8 NovLakshmi Puja: the main night of Diwali
Mon 9 NovGovardhan Puja (Annakut); in Gujarat, the new year
Tue 10 NovBhai Dooj: sisters pray for their brothers

Main Diwali night in the coming years: 29 Oct 2027 · 17 Oct 2028 · 5 Nov 2029 · 26 Oct 2030. Exact dates and puja times vary a little by region and panchang.

How it's celebrated
  • Cleaning and decorating the home, with rangoli at the door and toran of marigold and mango leaves.
  • Lighting diyas and kandils (lanterns), and the evening Lakshmi–Ganesh puja.
  • Sweets and gifts for family, friends and neighbours, such as laddoo, kaju katli and soan papdi.
  • New clothes, family get-togethers, and visits to elders for blessings.
Why it's good for us
  • Family and community: it brings families home and neighbours together across faiths.
  • A fresh start: the cleaning, repairs and new account books give the whole year a reset.
  • Livelihoods: potters who make diyas, florists, sweet-makers and small traders earn much of their year's income in this season.
  • Kindness: sharing sweets and helping those in need are part of the festival.
  • Hope: lighting a lamp against the darkest night of the month is a reminder that light always returns.
A green Diwali

Firecrackers became part of Diwali only in recent centuries. Their smoke adds to the worst air of the year in many Indian cities in late autumn, and their noise hurts babies, older people, patients and animals. Diyas, rangoli, lanterns, sweets and family time are the heart of the festival, and they don't pollute at all. If you do burst crackers, choose certified green crackers, follow local timings and keep water nearby.
